I visited an early polling centre in Kew, the seat of Liberal leader Jess Wilson, to collect how-to-vote cards from the parties in contention. Then I checked them against the preference flows in our model.

Card vs model

I compared the five main contenders only: Wilson (Liberal), Sun (Labor), Carter (Greens), Laird (One Nation) and independent Sophie Torney. Torney's card comes from the VEC register.

The model matches only One Nation on the next pick, but it agrees with all three cards that choose between Wilson and Torney. That's the comparison that matters most if Kew comes down to a Wilson–Torney final two.

The biggest miss is the Liberal card, which puts One Nation ahead of Torney. Our model sends Liberal votes towards Torney first because its flows are fitted to the 2022 counts. With the Liberals recommending One Nation this year, that assumption deserves a closer look.

Caveats

  • Cards aren't votes. Many voters ignore them, so they show what campaigns recommend, not where preferences will actually flow.
  • The model ranks groups of votes, not individual ballots. Its order is the most common ranking across 5,000 draws of the forecast's preference flows. Torney's row is especially uncertain.
  • This doesn't change the Kew forecast. The cards were not used as model inputs.
